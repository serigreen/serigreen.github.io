The problem

Silkworms are sensitive, and the field around them is not under the farmer's control.

Sericulture supports a large number of smallholder families in India. It is also unusually exposed — to a neighbour's spray schedule, to weather that no longer follows the old pattern, and to the simple absence of any record of what happened.

01

Drift arrives unannounced

Silkworms are acutely sensitive to pesticide residue. Spray drifting from an adjacent field can settle on mulberry leaf with nothing to warn the farm it happened — until the batch fails.

02

Narrow tolerances

Rearing succeeds only inside a tight band of temperature and humidity. An excursion at the wrong stage of the cycle can cost the entire crop.

03

Nothing to learn from

When a crop fails, most farms cannot say why. Without measurement there is no record to reason about and nothing to improve against next season.

How a batch is lost

The drift chain.

Six stages, none of them dramatic. The loss is decided long before anyone can see that anything went wrong.

01

The neighbour sprays

Cotton, sugarcane, banana, chilli — whatever is next door. India has no buffer-zone rule protecting sericulture, and he has no duty to tell anyone.

Surveyed
02

The fine droplets travel

Nozzles throw 50–500 µm. Anything under 150 µm does not fall. It stays up and moves with the wind.

Literature
03

It reaches the leaf

Direct drift, vapour settling out overnight, dew, soil to root. Several routes onto mulberry, not one.

Documented
04

Nothing looks wrong

The leaf is fresh and green; residue is invisible. Sericulture has a zero-tolerance standard, so a dose far below lethal has already ruined it.

The gap
05

He cuts, and he feeds

Within hours the worms stop feeding, regurgitate, curl, and go flaccid. By then the decision that mattered is days behind him.

Published toxicology
06

The cycle is gone

Death, or a worm that eats for eighteen days and never spins. Weeks of labour, leaf and eggs go with it.

What we put into stage 04

A unit on the boundary notices the air change and raises a score. It does not name the chemical and it does not touch the leaf. It tells you that a spray passed this point, at this time — so the decision it changes is whether you cut leaf from that block, and when.

Our approach

Instrument the boundary. Warn early. Improve on the record.

Our work follows the structure of our patent application — a field optimisation and protection system. Protection is where it starts, because that is where a season is most often lost.

WATCH

Sit where the drift arrives

Instruments on the boundary facing the neighbouring field, sampling the air and the wind that carry a spray onto mulberry leaf.

WARN

Alarm on a fast reading, not a high one

Plumes arrive in seconds; weather moves over hours. Reading the rate of change rather than the level is what separates a spray from an ordinary afternoon.

WAIT

Hold the cut, and keep the record

The decision this changes is whether to cut leaf from that block, and when. The record stays afterwards, to explain an outcome and to carry into the next season.
